I went into full meltdown with diarrhea on a cruise through the Mediterranean. The doctor on board was Austrian. She asked me ONE question, and suddenly my gastroenterologist back home seemed 15 years behind.

I'm 56.

My husband and I put money aside for two years for that trip. Fourteen days around the Mediterranean. Greece, Italy, Spain.

It's day four, and we're making our way through Santorini.

Narrow cobblestone lanes. Steep climbs. Gorgeous everywhere you look.

But my stomach is rolling. Four days in, and I'm rushing to a bathroom six, seven, eight times a day. The heavy food on the ship, the late meals, the whole routine thrown off, it all pushed my gut into overdrive. I'm so bloated that even my loosest clothes feel tight across my belly.

By the time we're back on board, I'm clenching every muscle I've got. The cramps are sharp. My blouse is soaked through with sweat. My stomach feels hard and swollen and I can feel the gas pushing against me from the inside.

My husband has that worried look. "Somebody needs to check that."

I don't want anybody checking my stomach. I haven't talked openly about the diarrhea in four years. Not with friends. Not with any doctor I could avoid. Not even with my husband, not really.

But it hurts too much to say no.

The next morning, I walk over to the concierge desk.

"I need to see a doctor about my stomach."

She picks up the phone. Books me in that afternoon with the ship's doctor.

The office is tiny. Spotless. Down on Deck 3, beside the medical center.

Dr. Weber. Austrian accent. Mid-50s, I'd guess.

She checks my abdomen. Presses gently down both sides. Has me lie back.

"Right now, the travel and the strange food are part of it," she tells me. "But this started long before the cruise. How long has the diarrhea been going on?"

"About five years," I tell her. "My gastroenterologist at home has me on Imodium. Every single morning. Just so I can leave the house."

"Has anyone ever tested you for bacterial overgrowth in your small intestine?"

I just look at her.

"What is that?"

She studies me for a second. Then puts her stethoscope down.

"Your gut doesn't go wrong for no reason," she says. "In a lot of chronic diarrhea that gets labeled IBS-D, what's really going on is too much bacteria in the small intestine, where it doesn't belong. And those bacteria don't just sit there. They build a protective layer around themselves, a slimy shield called biofilm. Underneath it, they're very hard to reach. They ferment your food before you've properly digested it. That makes gas and pulls water into your intestines. That's your urgency. That's the watery stool. That's why you can eat the plainest food in the world and your body still reacts."

"The bigger problem is what years of Imodium does when that's what's going on. Every tablet slows things down at the bottom, but up above, the bacteria keep fermenting under that shield. Keep making gas. Keep drawing water in. The overgrowth builds. The symptoms get worse between doses. You need more tablets. They do less. You're handling today's symptoms while the thing causing them keeps growing."

I'm following her. But I can't understand why my gastroenterologist never said a word about this.

"My gastroenterologist has never brought up bacterial overgrowth."

Dr. Weber nods, slowly.

"In America, it doesn't come up much," she says. "Most of them stick to anti-diarrhea pills, and then rifaximin if it gets worse. Rifaximin can knock back what's out in the open. But the shield protects the rest, and once you stop, they grow back. Where I trained, we look at what's hiding first. The bacteria aren't going anywhere until that shield comes down."

"So what am I supposed to do?" I ask.

"There's a lot of work on plants that get at that shield," she says. "Garlic is the first one I'd look at. The compound in it, allicin, has been shown in lab studies to help break that layer down. Once it's open, herbs like oregano and black walnut can reach what was hiding underneath. One herb on its own usually isn't enough. They work better together. And start slow. A few drops at first, then build up. When that shield opens up, you can feel it."

"My gastroenterologist never told me any of this."

"Where I come from, it's nothing unusual," she says. "Here, it's just not part of the routine yet. If I were you, I'd read up on it when you get home."

She stopped. Then said one more thing:

"Five years on a pill that handles today's symptoms isn't treatment. It's just watching it get worse."

I thank her and go.

But I can't get it out of my head.

For the rest of the trip, it keeps coming back to me.

Five years. My gastroenterologist kept prescribing Imodium. Ran my labs every few months. Watched me go from one tablet a day to two. Then to three.

And told me to keep taking it.

Not once did she tell me bacteria could be building up in my small intestine. That they could hide under a shield. That they were fermenting my food before my body had a chance to digest it.

11:45 PM.
